Heart rate & temperature

Take the heart rate straight after work, then again at 10 and 20 minutes, and log the temperature. A horse recovering slower than its usual, or running a temperature, can be an early sign of soreness, illness or fatigue. Fill in whichever readings you have.

Filming for a good read

Steady, close, well-lit footage on firm ground makes the biggest difference.

Every video

  • Hold the phone landscape and still, at about the height of the horse's elbow. Brace against a rail if you can.
  • Film on a firm, level surface. Soft ground hides lameness.
  • Keep the whole horse in frame, hooves included. Zoom as little as possible.
  • Record at 60 fps if your phone offers it, and 1080p rather than 4K (smaller files upload faster).
  • Get at least 6 to 8 strides of steady movement. Handler on a loose lead so the head can move freely.

By camera view

Towards / away
Jog 25 to 30 m straight away from you, turn, and come back. Stand directly in line. Best for head nod and hip hike.
Side-on
Stand 8 to 10 m to the side and let the horse pass. Film both sides in separate clips. Best for stride length and fetlock drop.
From behind
Stand still behind the horse as it moves away. Best for hip symmetry and how each hind foot lands.
In the cart
Film from the infield or a following vehicle, side-on or from behind. Note if hopples are on.
Lunging
Film both reins as separate checks, and say which rein in step 2. Lameness often shows more on one rein.

Pacers and trotters

Mark each horse's gait when you add it. Trotters move diagonal legs together and pacers move same-side legs together, so the head and hip clues read differently. For pacers, footage from behind and side-on usually reads more clearly than head-on.

Gait Check gives a second opinion from video to help you decide what to watch and when to call the vet. It does not replace a veterinary lameness exam, flexion tests or imaging.
